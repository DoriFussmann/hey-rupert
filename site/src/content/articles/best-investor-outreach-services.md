## The Short Answer

There is no single best investor outreach service. There's a best one for what you're short of. If you're short of **time**, use a managed service that builds the list and runs the outreach for you, such as Rupert, Waveup or Qubit Capital. If you're short of **budget**, use a free investor database like OpenVC with your own email and a simple CRM. If you want **introductions instead of outbound email**, look at a network-based service like Funden. And if you want help with everything from the deck to the term sheet, look at a broader advisory like Waveup's Full-Stack tier.

## What an Investor Outreach Service Actually Does

Every raise needs the same four jobs done, and services differ in which of them they take off your hands:

1. **Build the target list.** Filter investors by stage, sector, geography, check size and thesis, and find the right partner at each firm.
2. **Write the outreach.** A message that makes sense for that investor, not a template with the company name swapped in.
3. **Send and follow up.** In waves, on a schedule, for weeks.
4. **Handle replies and meetings.** Sort real interest from noise, prepare for each conversation, keep the process moving.

Software does part of job 1 and helps you organize the rest. Managed services do jobs 1 to 3, and some help with 4. Advisory firms add the work around the outreach: the deck, the model, the data room and the closing.

## Done-For-You Outreach

Choose this if the bottleneck is time: you know you should be contacting 100 investors and you've contacted 12.

**Rupert** runs a two-to-four-week setup (intake, narrative, expert feedback on the deck and materials, messaging, and investor matching from its own database, including the investors behind your funded competitors and the funds that resemble them), then sends from the founder's own inbox, with critical emails written for each investor and follow-up on a schedule. The founder approves everything before it goes live, and the fee is a setup fee plus a flat monthly fee, with [no success fee](/pricing/).

**Waveup**, operating since 2014, offers outreach in three tiers on its [pricing page](https://waveup.com/pricing/): a founder-run Foundations tier, a Managed tier where Waveup researches, writes, sends and follows up, and a Full-Stack tier that adds closing support. It also sells decks, models and valuation work, and says it charges a success fee only on a few engagements.

**Qubit Capital** pairs AI investor matching with a team that runs research, outreach and follow-up while the founder takes the meetings, and also prepares materials. It publishes no prices.

**Fundex**'s managed tier, run by Fidelman &amp; Co, handles targeting, campaigns and scheduling from $6,000 a month plus a 3% success fee.

## Investor Databases and Targeting Tools

Choose this if you have the time and want to keep costs down.

**OpenVC** is the main free option: an investor database with more than 10,000 investors (company-reported) and a deck-submission flow. According to its [help center](https://openvc.app/help), submissions go out from OpenVC's address, with replies coming to you; free users can send one a day and Premium users five, at $99 a month or $299 a year.

**Foundersuite** pairs a large investor database with a fundraising CRM, from a free tier up to about $149 a month.

Whichever you use, the database is raw material, not a list. Thousands of rows still have to be filtered to the investors who fit, put in order, written to and followed up, and that is where most of the value is won or lost. Budget the time honestly before choosing this route. Our guide to [building an investor list](/articles/investor-list/) covers how.

## CRMs and Pipeline Tools

Choose this if you're running the raise yourself and need to keep dozens of conversations straight.

**Foundersuite** is the established fundraising CRM, with pipelines, investor updates and a data room add-on. **Metal** is the AI-heavy alternative: it maps warm-introduction paths through your Gmail and LinkedIn, drafts outreach, and offers an Autopilot tier with hands-on help; [pricing](https://metal.so/pricing) is custom. **Fundex** also sells a CRM platform from $100 a month. For a wider comparison, see [the best investor CRMs](/articles/investor-crm/).

## Warm Introductions and Networks

Choose this if you'd rather be introduced than write cold.

**Funden** distributes your deal to its network of investment partners and makes double opt-in introductions, alongside coaching and materials review. **Metal** and **OpenVC Premium** help you find warm paths in your own network. Warm introductions convert well but are hard to get at scale, so most founders combine them with direct outreach rather than relying on them alone.

## What We've Learned Running Investor Outreach

Rupert's founder, Dori Fussmann, spent years in investment banking before building Rupert. Four views shape how Rupert works, and they're worth considering whichever option you choose.

### 1. A database isn't a pipeline

"Founders often buy investor databases without really knowing what they're buying. You get a spreadsheet with thousands of investors. Now what? How do you filter it, which investors are actually relevant, who do you approach first, and how do you track every conversation, follow-up and reply? The data is valuable, but without the experience, process and systems to work through it, much of that value goes to waste. And founders rarely have time to run a large outreach operation themselves. Buying a database is buying raw material. Someone still has to turn it into a working pipeline."

### 2. Outreach is a professional service, not a commission

"Coming from investment banking, I know success fees well. They're standard there. But investor outreach is a different service. We're responsible for identifying the right investors, matching, organizing and running the outreach. We're not the company, the product or the founders, and we don't control whether investors like the business or whether the valuation works. There's also proportionality: a company raises a few million dollars and a success fee for outreach can run to tens of thousands, which doesn't reflect the work involved. So we charge for the work, the expertise and the execution. The outcome belongs to the company."

### 3. Readiness comes before outreach

"I wouldn't steer founders toward doing outreach themselves. It's specialized and time-consuming, and it's exactly what Rupert was built to handle. But I will tell a founder when I think they're not ready to raise. Sometimes the product, traction, business model or overall maturity doesn't support the round yet, and no amount of matching, outreach or clever messaging will fix that. We can get you in front of the right investors. We can't make your company investable."

### 4. Matching beats volume

"Running real campaigns teaches you things no database or software can. You learn which approaches work, which investors are responsive and which are hard to engage, and how much fit matters: approaching irrelevant funds wastes everyone's time. Different funds have different strategies and criteria, and you need to know what each one is looking for and which parts of the company matter most to them. Outreach isn't about sending as many emails as possible. It's about knowing who to approach, why they're relevant, and how to introduce the company in a way that lands with that investor."

## How to Run Outreach That Doesn't Read Like Mass Email

Whatever you choose, the difference between outreach that gets answered and outreach that gets ignored comes down to a few habits:

1. **Filter for fit first.** Stage, sector, geography and check size, before brand or fund size.
2. **Write to a person.** The partner who covers your space, not the firm's general inbox.
3. **Make it specific.** Reference what they've backed and why you fit. If the email could go to any investor, rewrite it.
4. **Send in waves.** Start with the investors you'd most like to hear from, learn from the replies, then widen.
5. **Follow up on a schedule.** Many replies come after a follow-up, not to the first email.
6. **Keep it in your name.** Investors respond to founders. Outreach from your own inbox keeps the relationship, and the history, with you.

## Key Takeaways

- Pick by what you're short of: time (managed outreach), budget (free databases and DIY), or access (introductions).
- Managed services take over list-building, writing, sending and follow-up; software leaves those to you.
- Published costs range from free (OpenVC) to about $10,000 a month (Waveup Managed); several providers price after a call.
- Success fees vary: none at Rupert, a few engagements at Waveup, 3% at Fundex's managed service.
- A database is raw material: the value comes from filtering, prioritizing and working it, which takes time and experience.
- Outreach can't fix a company that isn't ready to raise. Check readiness first.
- Good outreach is about fit, not volume: specific, sent in waves, followed up on a schedule, and sent in the founder's own name.
