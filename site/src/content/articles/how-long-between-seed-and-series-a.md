When founders ask how long it takes to go from a seed round to a Series A, they are usually asking the wrong question. The right question — the one that actually shapes how you should run your company for the next 20 months — is: how long do you need to build the kind of business a Series A investor will fund? Those are not the same thing, and confusing them is one of the most expensive planning mistakes in early-stage startups.

## The Number Every Founder Needs to Internalize

The median time between closing a seed round and closing a Series A has stretched to 616 days, or about 20 months, per Carta's Q2 2025 data — up from roughly 420 days in 2021.
 That 84% increase is not noise. 
Investors have raised the bar on what a company needs to show before they'll underwrite a Series A — more revenue, better retention, cleaner unit economics — so companies need more runway to hit those numbers before they even start pitching.

The implications for runway planning are stark. 
Seed rounds are still sized against an 18 to 24 month assumption, but the median company now waits closer to two years — and a growing share waits three. Ask a founder how much runway a seed round should buy and most will say eighteen months, maybe twenty-four if they have raised before. Raising a seed round sized for a 12-month runway when the market median to reach Series A is closer to 20 months is a self-inflicted bridge round waiting to happen.

There is also a sobering graduation rate behind the headline. 
Round sizes and valuations are the highest on record according to Carta, but only 15–20% of seed-funded startups raise an institutional Series A within 24 months — less than half the graduation rate seen for cohorts through 2020.

39% of startups now take three or more years to go from seed to Series A, according to Carta's 2025 data.
 These figures are not reasons to despair; they are reasons to plan precisely.

## The Two Timelines You Are Conflating

The most clarifying thing you can do for your Series A strategy is to separate the timeline in your head into two distinct phases with very different lengths. "How long does a Series A take" has two completely different answers depending on whether you mean the sprint (pitching to close) or the marathon (seed close to Series A close), and conflating the two is why so many founders budget 6 months of runway for a process that actually takes 20.

The sprint — the active fundraising process — is well-documented. 
The typical active fundraising process, from first investor pitch to money in the bank, takes 4–9 months in 2026. Fast closes of 4–5 months are possible for companies with exceptional metrics and strong warm investor relationships already in place.

For most founders, budgeting 6–8 months is more realistic.
 That process runs through distinct phases: 
4–6 weeks of prep, 8–12 weeks of active pitching, and 4–8 weeks of diligence and legal closing.

The marathon — the time building toward Series A readiness — is what most founders underestimate. 
The 6–9 month number describes the sprint once you've decided to run the process; it says nothing about how long you'll spend building toward the metrics that make investors want to take the meeting in the first place.

## What the Bar Actually Looks Like in 2026

Understanding the timeline requires understanding what you are building toward. Series A investors in 2026 are not funding potential; they are funding evidence.

The current ARR benchmark for a competitive Series A raise in B2B SaaS generally starts at $2 million to $5 million in ARR, equivalent to roughly $167K to $417K in monthly recurring revenue, with top-quartile companies materially higher. Median revenue at Series A reached $2.5 million in 2025, roughly 75 percent higher than in 2021.
 Revenue quality matters as much as the number itself. 
Efficient growth now earns a higher valuation than hypergrowth, which means a company growing steadily with strong retention often gets a better reception than one growing faster while burning through cash.

Series A investors typically look for 15–20% month-over-month growth sustained over at least six months. Consistency matters more than peak numbers — a company growing 15% every month is more fundable than one with one 40% spike followed by flat months.
 On retention, 
the 18 to 28-month window has become the realistic range for priced Series A rounds in 2026, and the real gate is not time but traction: $2M+ ARR, 3x growth, 110%+ NRR, and a repeatable GTM motion.

The investor relationship dimension is equally important and just as frequently overlooked. 
Two of every three Series A deals now involve investors who already knew the founder for 6–9+ months before the round opened. The cold-start round — walk in with a deck and walk out with a term sheet — is much harder outside of repeat founders with proven exits.

## A Milestone Sequencing Framework: Month by Month

The 616-day median is not a passive waiting period. It is a building period, and the founders who use it well are the ones who arrive at their formal Series A process with both the metrics and the investor relationships already in place. Here is how to think about that window.

### Months 0–3: Foundation Before Traction

The weeks immediately after closing your seed round feel like momentum, but they are actually the highest-leverage window for structural decisions. This is the time to lock in your north-star metrics for the Series A — what ARR, retention, and growth rate will you need to demonstrate, and what does the path to each look like? Hire the first two or three people who will drive those numbers, and resist the temptation to staff up faster than your revenue thesis supports. Set up your financial infrastructure now: accounting, a real financial model, and basic revenue tracking that will eventually feed your data room. The decisions you make in months 0–3 about burn rate, hiring sequencing, and product focus will determine whether month 16 is a confident fundraise or a desperate one.

### Months 4–12: Building the First Customer Wave

This phase is entirely about acquiring paying customers and establishing that people will pay for your product at market rates — and will keep paying. 
Traction at this stage is not about momentum alone. It is about whether progress feels durable, repeatable, and understandable under scrutiny.
 Investors will reconstruct your cohort retention, your CAC against different acquisition channels, and your expansion revenue behavior. Build with that diligence in mind from day one. Three to five reference customers who can speak expansively about why they bought and why they would not leave are worth more than twenty logo-count customers whose names you can barely remember.

### Months 12–16: Proving the Growth Engine

By the one-year mark you should have enough data to know whether you have a repeatable go-to-market motion or a founder-led sales process that breaks if you try to replicate it. These are very different things, and Series A investors will probe for the difference. This is the phase to prove that your acquisition model works without the founding team closing every deal. Begin building your financial infrastructure in earnest: a three-year model with scenario analysis, monthly board reporting, and cohort analysis that shows you understand why customers stay, expand, and churn. 
The founders who clear Series A in 2026 share three traits: they hit the efficiency metrics (burn multiple, NRR, LTV/CAC), they built investor relationships before they needed capital, and they can articulate a repeatable GTM motion — not just a revenue number.

### Months 16–20+: Preparing to Launch the Formal Process

The single biggest planning error at this stage is treating "Series A preparation" as something that happens in the final month before you start taking meetings. 
Starting too late is the single biggest fundraising error.
 Preparation for a Series A — data room, financial model, cohort analysis, investor target list — should begin at least six months before you intend to run a formal process. That means starting no later than the 12–14 month post-seed mark for most founders. The actual outreach campaign, when you launch it, should feel like the culmination of months of preparation, not the beginning of it.

## Why Extending Runway Beats Raising Under Pressure

One counterintuitive implication of the 616-day median is that founders who deliberately push past the 18-month assumption and build toward genuinely stronger metrics consistently close faster and on better terms than those who rush the process.

Companies that raised $30 million-plus Series A rounds between Q1 2025 and Q1 2026 closed in roughly 14–15 months from their seed, while those raising $0–10 million took roughly 22 months. That's not a random spread — it's investors rewarding companies that hit strong metrics quickly with both a faster close and a bigger check, while slower-growing companies wait longer and often raise less when they do get there.

The mechanism is simple: a company at $3M ARR with strong NRR and a clean burn multiple creates competitive dynamics among investors. A company at $800K ARR with spotty retention and a narrative-heavy deck creates optionality for investors to wait. 
Strong companies still raise quickly and on good terms. The change is that a polished narrative no longer covers for thin numbers, and a thin process no longer reaches enough investors to find the ones who say yes.

Bridge rounds have become a real feature of the landscape for founders navigating this window — a structural response to the widening gap between seed size and Series A readiness. 
Carta reported that 16.6% of all cash raised by startups on Carta in Q2 2025 came through bridge rounds, up from 11.8% a year earlier, while 46% of seed deals in Q1 2025 were bridge rounds.
 A bridge is not a failure, but it should be treated as a deliberate strategic choice tied to specific milestones, not as a soft landing when the timeline slips. 
The most effective founders are reframing bridge rounds from a sign of weakness to a deliberate strategy. Rather than waiting until cash runs out, they proactively approach existing investors with a clear plan: specific milestones to hit, a defined timeline, and a credible path to a priced round. This approach maintains investor confidence and avoids the desperate dynamics that lead to punitive terms.

## The Preparation Clock Starts Before the Fundraise Does

Given that the active Series A process takes 4–9 months, and that most founders need at least 6 weeks of preparation before their first formal pitch, the practical implication is clear: if you want to close your Series A by month 20 post-seed, you need to start building your outreach infrastructure and investor target list by month 13 or 14 at the latest.

This is where most founders lose time they cannot recover. Researching the right investors, building a warm pipeline through conference introductions and LP referrals, and crafting a pitch calibrated to where your company actually is — all of this takes months of consistent effort. 
Start the investor relationship work 12 months before your target close.
 That means the preparation clock starts when your seed round closes, not when you decide you are ready to raise.

When you are 4–6 months out from launching your formal Series A process, the infrastructure question becomes urgent. The founders who close in 4–5 months are the ones who already have a curated list of investors who fund their stage and sector, warm introductions to the most relevant ones, and a pitch calibrated to investor pattern-matching in their category. Building that infrastructure from scratch once you are already in market adds months you cannot afford. ## Key Takeaways

- The median time between a seed close and a Series A close is now approximately 616 days (roughly 20 months), up 84% from 420 days in 2021 — founders who plan seed runway around an 18-month assumption are systematically under-capitalised.
- There are two distinct timelines: the 4–9 month active fundraising sprint and the much longer period of building the metrics that earn a first meeting; conflating them is the single most common and costly planning mistake.
- The Series A bar in 2026 requires $2M–$5M ARR, consistent 15–20% month-over-month growth, NRR above 110%, and a demonstrably repeatable GTM motion — evidence of a business, not just a promising team.
- Founders who deliberately extend runway to hit stronger metrics close faster and raise more: companies that raised $30M+ Series A rounds closed in roughly 14–15 months post-seed, while those raising under $10M took closer to 22 months.
- Start building investor relationships and Series A preparation materials — data room, financial model, cohort analysis — at least 6 months before you intend to formally launch the process, not the month before your first pitch.
