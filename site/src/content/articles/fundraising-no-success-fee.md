## Why a Success Fee Misaligns Your Advisor With You

There is a logic to success fees that sounds compelling at first: the advisor only gets paid if you close, so their interests must be aligned with yours. It sounds fair, even founder-friendly. But the alignment breaks in ways that matter, and the break is structural, not circumstantial.

Whenever an advisor ties their compensation to a financial outcome connected to your company, misalignment is baked in—not a risk, a certainty. With fundraising, this plays out in a specific and consequential way. I have seen founders rationalize it as a "beggars can't be choosers" situation: if someone wants to bring in capital, you take it. But that reasoning has a real cost. Sometimes you end up compromising—accepting money from the wrong investor, or raising the wrong amount—because the conflict of interest between you and a percentage-based advisor was never fully visible until it was too late. Getting money from the wrong investor can cost far more than the fee ever would. The solution is structurally simple: a fixed fee means your advisor's focus is on running a good process, not on optimizing their own percentage.

Here is exactly how the misalignment operates in practice. A success fee is a percentage of total capital raised, so an advisor earns more from a larger round regardless of whether a larger round is right for the company. The advisor has a financial incentive to push for the fastest close rather than the best terms or the most compatible investors. That is not a character flaw—it is what the incentive structure produces. Success fees for raises in the $5M–$10M range commonly fall between 3% and 6% of capital raised , and that percentage is often layered on top of a monthly retainer. A 5% success fee alone equals $250,000 —capital that is not going into the business. If your advisor earns a percentage of whatever you raise, their calculus on "good enough" and yours are different from the moment you sign.

There is also the tail clause problem. Even if you cancel a success-fee agreement, many contracts obligate you to pay the percentage on investors introduced during the engagement for an extended period after it ends. A check that arrives long after you stopped working together can still trigger a substantial payment. Read any success-fee agreement carefully before signing; the tail clause is where founders most often get caught.

A third issue is relationship ownership. When an advisor sends outreach under their own name or through their own domain, the investor knows the advisor—not you. Every conversation is filtered through an intermediary. When you stop working with that advisor, those investor relationships do not transfer. You have paid for introductions that do not belong to you. With a flat-fee model where outreach runs from your inbox and under your name, the investor knows you from the first message, and those relationships remain yours regardless of what comes next.

## How This Compares to Other Types of Fundraising Help

Flat-fee capital raising is one model in a market with several. Understanding the differences helps you decide which fits your situation, rather than defaulting to whatever you encounter first.

**Success-fee advisors and placement agents** typically charge a retainer plus a percentage of capital raised, and success fees usually run 2–6% of the round closed, often layered on top of a retainer . Advisors running this model usually conduct outreach under their own name, sitting between you and the investor. The relationship is theirs, the percentage is theirs, and the tail clause may follow you for some time after you stop working together.

**Fundraising consultants on retainer** charge for advice and help with materials—narrative, deck feedback, investor targeting strategy—but leave the founder to run most of the actual outreach. The monthly fee buys expertise and guidance, not execution. If your calendar is already stretched, a consulting relationship adds to your workload rather than relieving it.

**Self-serve software**—databases and CRMs, such as Foundersuite—gives you access to investor contact information and pipeline management tools, but you research, write, and follow up on everything. These tools are useful when you have the time and discipline to use them systematically. Most founders who are also running a company do not.

## How to Avoid Paying a Success Fee on Your Raise

If avoiding a success fee matters to you, the steps are straightforward—but they require reading agreements carefully before signing anything. Before signing any fundraising agreement, check whether it includes a success fee, a percentage of capital raised, or an equity component. If alignment matters to you, refuse any that do. This applies equally to advisors, placement agents, and any service that describes itself as contingent on closing. Some agreements obscure the percentage structure in definitions of "compensation" or "placement fees"—read the full document, not just the summary.

Read the tail clause specifically. Even if you cancel, some agreements require you to pay the percentage on investors introduced during the engagement for an extended period after it ends. Know exactly which investors are covered, for how long, and whether any exclusions apply. Negotiate the tail period before you sign if the standard clause is longer than you can accept.

Confirm that outreach will go from your own inbox and under your own name—not the advisor's. This protects your relationships regardless of who you work with in the future. An advisor who runs outreach under their own brand is building their network with your company as the vehicle. Choose a month-to-month engagement so you can stop paying the moment the outreach effort is no longer delivering value.
