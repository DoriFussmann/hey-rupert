There are three frameworks every well-read founder has heard of, and most founders treat them as interchangeable alternatives for the same job. They are not. The Sequoia [pitch deck](/articles/pitch-deck/) template, the Y Combinator format, and Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule were each designed for a different audience, a different delivery method, and a different moment in a company's story. Choosing the wrong one doesn't just produce a weaker deck — it signals to investors that the founder doesn't understand how the fundraising process actually works.

## What the Sequoia Framework Actually Is

Sequoia Capital's published pitch framework is the closest thing the startup industry has to a default template — and importantly, it is not a deck. It is a checklist of 10 questions every deck should answer.

[Sequoia Capital published a guide on how to write a business plan](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/) and pitch a startup as part of their longstanding founder-facing content, and the 10-section framework that emerged from it has, over the past two decades, become the closest thing the startup industry has to a universally accepted default. As Sequoia Capital publishes it today, the template is: Company purpose, Problem, Solution, Why now, Market potential, Competition and alternatives, Business model, Team, Financials, Vision.

Worth noting: Sequoia's original page actually lists its prompts ending on Vision, with no standalone "Product" section — the popular template most founders use swapped Product in for Vision.
 Either way, the canonical count is ten sections, and the sequence is not accidental.

The framework's real power is in that sequence. Company Purpose forces the founder to compress their entire thesis into a single declarative sentence before they can move on. Problem anchors the deck in a specific customer's pain rather than a market abstraction. Solution describes the eureka moment. And then comes the section that separates the Sequoia framework from nearly every generic alternative: Why Now.

### Why the "Why Now" Slide Is the Framework's Defining Feature

The Why Now slide asks for the market shift — regulation, technology, behaviour change — why previous attempts failed, and what's different this time.
 This slide has no equivalent in most generic pitch templates, and its presence in the Sequoia framework is deliberate. Venture funds make bets on timing as much as on products. A founder who can articulate a crisp "why now" unlock — a regulatory change, an API that just became available, a cost curve that just crossed a threshold — is telling a VC partner that they understand the structural conditions that make a company fundable, not just interesting. Founders who have a genuine, defensible answer to this question will find the Sequoia framework amplifies their narrative better than any other structure.

Unlike a demo-day format, the Sequoia template isn't time-constrained. It's designed for the deck you send to investors before a meeting — the one they'll spend 3–5 minutes reviewing to decide if they want to talk.
 That distinction matters enormously when choosing between frameworks, and it's where the comparison with Y Combinator and Guy Kawasaki sharpens.

## The Y Combinator Format: Brevity as a Proof Standard

YC's approach focuses on clarity over complexity. A YC pitch deck is usually short — around 10 to 12 slides — and structured for fast reading, with every slide supporting one idea and leading naturally to the next.
 Where the Sequoia framework is a document with narrative depth, the YC format treats each slide as a single evidentiary claim. The philosophy is almost adversarial toward founders who have not yet earned the right to a long deck.

The YC [pitch deck template](/articles/startup-pitch-deck-template/) is often considered the gold standard for startup fundraising, and its minimalist approach cuts through the noise by focusing on the core elements investors prioritize: problem, solution, business model, and traction.
 That last word — traction — is where the YC format departs most sharply from the Sequoia structure. In practice, YC's guidance pushes traction evidence earlier and harder than most frameworks. 
The most persuasive YC-style submissions tell a tight, data-driven story that begins with a well-defined problem and a differentiated solution, then anchors that narrative to credible traction, scalable unit economics, and a clear path to rapid but disciplined expansion. [According to YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck), a great pitch deck follows three fundamental principles: Legibility, Simplicity, and Obviousness.

YC emphasizes that slides should be readable even from the back of the room — large font sizes, no paragraphs, minimal text.
 These are not aesthetic preferences. They are a signal that every claim on every slide should be so clear it doesn't require a narrating founder to make sense of it. That principle makes the YC format the strongest choice when traction is the deck's most compelling argument: the numbers speak loudly, and the format is designed to let them.

### When to Choose the YC Format Over Sequoia

The practical decision point is this: if a founder's most powerful slide is a traction chart — consistent month-over-month revenue growth, strong retention cohorts, a marquee customer logo — the YC format puts that evidence in front of investors faster. The Sequoia framework, by contrast, builds the narrative context first (purpose, problem, solution, why now) and arrives at proof later. For a pre-seed company raising on vision and team, that narrative runway is valuable. For a seed-stage company with $40K MRR and a clear wedge, the YC format's evidence-first rhythm is more persuasive.

## Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 Rule: A Live Pitch Standard

Guy Kawasaki's rule is simple: a pitch should have ten slides, last no more than twenty minutes, and contain no font smaller than thirty points — a rule applicable for any presentation to reach an agreement, including raising capital. He posted the 10/20/30 rule on his blog in 2005,
 and it has remained useful for exactly one context: the live, in-room presentation where the founder is present and the clock is running.

The rule is designed to keep presentations focused, engaging, and easily digestible for the audience. By limiting the number of slides and presentation time, it forces entrepreneurs to distill their ideas to their essence and cut the fluff.
 The 30-point font minimum is the rule's most underappreciated constraint: 
big type forces you to know your material rather than read dense slides, and it keeps each slide to a single point.

The critical limitation of the Kawasaki framework — one that many founders miss — is embedded in its own logic. 
A technical workshop, a self-contained reading deck, and a live sales conversation may need different formats. The decision about what the document is for should come before applying the numbers, because a deck that works when you speak may not provide enough detail when forwarded without you.
 This is the 10/20/30 rule's fundamental constraint as a fundraising tool: it is an in-room performance framework, not an emailed document framework. A deck built entirely to these constraints — minimal text, 30-point type, 20-minute pacing — will leave an investor who opens it asynchronously with insufficient context to form a view.

For demo day presentations, live partner pitches, and accelerator showcase events, the Kawasaki discipline is genuinely useful and often underused. For the outbound email deck that gets forwarded among a firm's partners before a meeting is ever booked, it is the wrong primary framework.

## Named Frameworks Signal Investor-Literacy

There is a less-discussed reason to understand these frameworks beyond their structural merits: using a named, credible framework tells an experienced investor something about the founder. 
An investor deck isn't a design project; it's a thinking tool. Sequoia Capital's 10-slide template is the best-known framework because it forces ruthless clarity about the business.

A founder who opens their deck with a crisp company purpose sentence, follows it with a specific customer pain, articulates a defensible "why now," and builds to a coherent business model is demonstrating that they have thought through the same questions a VC partner uses to evaluate deals. A founder who opens with a generic mission statement on a visually busy slide — regardless of how polished the design looks — signals the opposite. The framework, used properly, is not just a structure. It is a demonstration of fundraising literacy.

This matters in practice because 
investor preferences have shifted significantly — decision-makers now prioritize concise storytelling, clear demonstration of market traction, and strong, defensible differentiation. They want to see not just a great idea, but evidence of real-world demand and a unique advantage.
 Any of the three frameworks, applied with discipline, can satisfy those expectations. A generic design template with no underlying argumentative logic cannot.

## Matching Framework to Stage and Format

The right framework is not a matter of personal preference — it is a function of three variables: raise stage, primary distribution format, and the company's strongest proof point. **At pre-seed**, the Sequoia framework typically wins. The Why Now section gives founders the narrative space to explain the structural unlock that makes this the right moment to build the company, even without revenue to show. The team slide carries more weight at this stage, and the Sequoia structure positions the team at slide eight — after the market and business model have established the size of the prize worth pursuing.

**At seed**, the choice depends on traction. If the company has meaningful revenue, retention data, or clear product-market fit signals, the YC format's evidence-first rhythm gets those signals in front of investors more quickly. If the company is raising on product vision and an early waitlist, the Sequoia framework's narrative depth serves better.

**At Series A**, the Sequoia framework or a hybrid of the two structured approaches is standard. 
The most compelling decks present three pillars with rigor: a credible market thesis supported by early user or customer validation; a repeatable sales or acquisition mechanism that indicates scalable unit economics; and a team composition that demonstrates complementary skills and domain knowledge.
 Both Sequoia and YC frameworks accommodate all three pillars; the Kawasaki framework was not designed for this level of analytical depth.

**For emailed decks**, neither the Kawasaki format nor an over-minimal YC-style deck works well without a presenting founder. The Sequoia framework's narrative structure was built for asynchronous reading — a partner can move through the 10 sections and reconstruct the thesis without a voice-over.

**For live pitches and demo days**, the Kawasaki discipline applied to whichever framework the founder is using is the right move: tighten the slides to one claim each, enforce the 20-minute constraint, and cut anything that requires the deck to explain itself.

## The Decision That Comes After the Framework

Choosing the right pitch deck framework is genuinely consequential — it shapes how investors read a company's story, and a mismatched framework can cost a compelling company its first meeting. But for most founders raising Seed or Series A capital, the framework decision is resolved in a single afternoon. The harder, more time-consuming problem is everything that comes after: identifying which investors are actually a fit for the company's stage, sector, and check size; building a targeted outreach list; personalizing each approach; and managing the pipeline of conversations without letting any of it collapse into a spreadsheet that stops getting updated two weeks in.

That targeting and outreach process is where most fundraises lose momentum — not because the deck was wrong, but because the right deck never reached the right investors. ## Key Takeaways

- The Sequoia pitch deck template — 10 sections from Company Purpose through Vision — is the industry's closest thing to a default VC framework and works best for founders with a clear "why now" narrative, particularly in emailed outbound decks sent before any meeting is booked.
- The YC format prioritizes brevity and hard evidence over narrative depth, making it the strongest choice at the seed stage when traction data is the deck's most compelling argument.
- Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30pt font minimum) is an in-room presentation framework first — its constraints are valuable for demo days and live pitches but leave too little context for an asynchronously read emailed deck.
- Using a named, credible framework signals investor-literacy to VC partners; it shows the founder understands the questions a fund uses to evaluate deals, before a single conversation takes place.
- The right framework depends on raise stage, distribution format (email vs. live pitch), and the company's strongest proof point — no single template is universally optimal across every fundraising scenario.
