Fundraising is one of the most time-intensive projects a founder will ever run—and unlike most projects, it competes directly with the job you were actually hired to do: build and grow the company. That tension is not a footnote. It is the central problem of the startup fundraising process, and the founders who handle it best are the ones who treat fundraising as a discipline with defined phases, defined outputs, and defined owners—not as a series of coffee meetings that eventually produce capital.

There are two reasons to think carefully about who does the work. The first is expertise: fundraising is a skill set that encompasses investor research, narrative construction, pipeline management, outreach personalization, and negotiation. Most founders are not trained in it, and learning on the job means making expensive mistakes in front of the investors you most want to impress. The second reason is time and attention: months spent on investor outreach, follow-up, and pipeline management are months not spent on product, customers, and growth. The discipline of the process is inseparable from the question of who owns it.

This guide walks through every phase of the fundraising process explained from start to close—what each phase requires, what output it produces, and what goes wrong when it is rushed or skipped. Whether you are a first-time founder trying to understand how to run a fundraising process, or preparing for a Series A, the same logic applies: a founder who understands the process before it starts is harder to stall, easier to compete with, and less likely to run out of runway mid-raise.

## Questions Founders Ask Before They Start

Before the five phases, four questions come up almost universally. Answering them directly prevents the most common early missteps.

**What is the startup fundraising process step by step?** At its simplest, the process runs in five sequential phases: preparation, investor targeting, outreach, meetings and diligence, and close. Each phase has a defined output. Skipping one means the next one stalls. The full process from first outreach to wired funds typically takes three to five months for a well-prepared founder at Seed, and longer at Series A.

**How do I understand the full fundraising process before I start?** Read this article in its entirety before you contact a single investor. The phases that hurt founders most—poor preparation, untargeted investor lists, inconsistent follow-up—are all preventable if you understand the structure in advance. The worst time to learn what a data room is or why list depth matters is during an active raise.

**How do I run a structured fundraising process as a first-time founder?** Structure comes from treating each phase as a project with a defined deliverable. Preparation ends when you have a fundable narrative and investor-ready materials. Targeting ends when you have a ranked list the founder has approved. Outreach runs on a schedule, not on inspiration. First-time founders most often struggle with the outreach phase because it requires consistency over weeks, not a single burst of effort—and because it must continue while the company is still operating.

**How do I run a competitive Series A process?** A Series A process becomes competitive when you have enough qualified investors in parallel conversations that no single fund can afford to move slowly. The goal is not to collect meetings—it is to create enough simultaneous momentum that a lead investor feels genuine pressure to move. That requires a deep enough target list, coordinated outreach timing, and disciplined follow-up so conversations do not drift out of sync. Each of these is developed fully in the phases below.

## The Five Phases of the Startup Fundraising Process

The startup fundraising process runs in five sequential phases: preparation, investor targeting, outreach, investor meetings and diligence, and close. Each phase has a defined output. Skipping or rushing an early phase is the most common reason a raise stalls in a later one. **The five phases and their outputs at a glance:**

| Phase | Output |
|---|---|
| 1. Preparation | Fundable narrative, materials, and tailored messaging |
| 2. Investor Targeting | A ranked, founder-approved investor list |
| 3. Outreach | Active conversations in a managed pipeline |
| 4. Meetings and Diligence | A lead term sheet |
| 5. Close | Wired capital |

Carta's data shows the median interval between a seed round and a Series A reached 616 days—roughly 20 months—in Q2 2025, more than two months longer than the median wait time two years prior. That figure is a useful anchor for how founders should think about timeline at every stage: the fundraising cycle is not just the active raise, but includes the time needed to reach the milestones that make the next round fundable.

## Phase 1: Preparation — What You Need Before You Contact a Single Investor

Preparation is the phase first-time founders most often underinvest in, and the one they most often blame the market for when outreach underperforms. The correlation is not coincidental.

Before outreach, you need a clear round thesis: how much you are raising, on what terms, and what the capital will specifically accomplish. Vagueness on any of those three points is immediately apparent to an experienced investor and signals that the founder has not done the internal work required to be a credible counterpart. The deck, one-pager, and financial model must answer the questions a target investor will ask, not just describe the company. A deck that explains what the company does is not the same as a deck that makes the case for why this business, at this valuation, with this team, deserves capital at this moment.

Messaging should also be tailored by investor type. A pre-seed angel hears a different emphasis than a Series A lead, even for the same company. Early-stage angels are often backing the founder's conviction and the size of the opportunity. Institutional Series A investors are evaluating whether a repeatable growth model exists and whether the unit economics can support the return profile their fund requires. Writing one version of your outreach email and sending it to both groups is one of the most reliable ways to reach neither.

The preparation phase should take two to four weeks if approached seriously. Done right, it means your deck has been reviewed and given ranked, honest feedback—not polite encouragement. It means your financial model has been stress-tested against the questions you will get in a second meeting. It means you have a crisp answer to "what does this round do for you?" that is specific enough to be memorable. Done poorly, preparation becomes a week of slide adjustments followed by premature outreach that teaches you what was missing through investor silence.

## Phase 2: Investor Targeting — How to Build a List Worth Working

A fundable investor list is not a list of famous funds. It is a list of investors who have a structural reason to say yes to your specific deal. That means filtering by stage fit, sector, geography, financing type, and current fund cycle—not by name recognition or who funded the company you admire most.

A well-constructed targeting process runs in passes. The first pass filters by the structural variables: does this fund invest at your stage, in your sector, in your geography, with the instrument you are issuing? The second pass looks for investors who have backed your direct competitors or companies that closely resemble yours in business model, sector adjacency, or customer type. That second pass is often the most valuable because it surfaces investors who have already done the thesis work on your market. A fund that has backed two companies solving adjacent problems has demonstrated conviction in the space. A cold email that opens by referencing one of their portfolio companies—and explaining why your approach is differentiated—reads very differently from a generic outreach.

List depth matters for a specific structural reason. A narrow pipeline cannot create the competitive pressure that leads to a term sheet. This is especially true for Series A processes, where institutional investors know when they are the only firm in the room and price accordingly. Running parallel conversations across a deep enough list is not aggressive—it is how the process is supposed to work. A list that starts at 80 to 120 qualified names, filtered and ranked by fit, gives you the depth to run real competition even after accounting for funds that are between cycles, have passed for fund-mandate reasons, or simply do not respond.

Every investor on the list should be approved by the founder before outreach begins. This is not a formality. It ensures no message goes somewhere the founder would not want it to go, preserves the founder's right to manage every relationship on their own terms, and prevents the kind of miscommunication that happens when an intermediary sends outreach to an investor the founder knows personally through a different context.

## Phase 3: Outreach — How to Run It Without Burning Relationships

Outreach is where the preparation work either pays off or exposes its gaps. The mechanics of running structured outreach explained simply: every initial email should be written for the specific investor, follow-up should run on a schedule rather than when the founder remembers, and nothing should go from a name the investor cannot trace back to the company.

The most consequential structural decision in outreach is whose inbox it comes from. Outreach sent from the founder's own email address preserves the relationship: the investor is in a conversation with the company, not with a representative who may or may not still be involved after the round closes. When an advisor or intermediary sends outreach under their own name, every conversation belongs to them. If the relationship between the founder and the advisor ends, so does the access.

Cold outreach works when it is specific, credible, and timely. A message that opens by referencing the investor's portfolio, articulates what the company does in one sentence, states the round clearly, and asks for a meeting is a good cold email. A message that uses the word "disruptive," includes five paragraphs about the total addressable market, and addresses the investor as "Dear Investor" is not. Generic or high-volume automated campaigns do not just underperform—they damage the founder's reputation with the investors they most want to reach, because anyone who receives a mass-produced message knows immediately what they are looking at.

Follow-up is where most outreach pipelines fail—and not through rejection. They fail through silence. An investor who has not replied to a first email is not necessarily uninterested; they are busy, managing a full inbox, and working through a screening process you cannot see from the outside. A polite, specific follow-up sent on a schedule is not pestering. It is the professional behavior an experienced investor expects from a founder who is running a real process. Building and maintaining that schedule manually while also running product reviews, hiring, and customer conversations is the operational problem the fundraising process creates—and the clearest argument for getting structured help before the outreach phase begins.

## Phase 4: Meetings and Diligence — Running a Competitive Process

The goal of a first investor meeting is not a term sheet. It is a second meeting. That reframe matters because it changes how you structure the conversation. A first meeting is primarily about whether the investor finds the opportunity interesting enough to spend more time on it. The job is to present clearly, answer questions directly, and earn the right to a deeper conversation—not to close.

Once second meetings start, you are in the phase where diligence begins in earnest. Diligence requests signal serious interest, and having a data room ready before you are asked shortens the cycle and signals operational maturity. A data room that is disorganized, incomplete, or assembled under time pressure communicates the opposite. For the Series A fundraising process specifically, institutional investors will check your metrics, your references, and your cap table before issuing a term sheet. Each of those should be clean before the process starts—a cap table with structural problems does not get cleaner when a lead investor's lawyer starts reviewing it.

Running multiple conversations in parallel is what creates the competition that produces a lead and sets terms. This is how the Series A fundraising process is supposed to work: you are not asking investors to evaluate you in isolation. You are running a process in which the investors who move fastest and most thoughtfully earn the opportunity to lead. Parallel conversations also protect you against the most common late-stage stall—an investor who is "almost there" and keeps scheduling one more meeting. When that investor knows three other funds are in advanced conversations, the timeline compresses. When they think they have infinite time, they take it.

## Phase 5: Close — What Happens After a Term Sheet Arrives

A term sheet is the beginning of the legal and financial close process, not the end of the fundraise. Founders who treat a signed term sheet as a closed round have made a mistake that sometimes corrects itself—and sometimes costs them the raise.

The key close steps, in order: negotiating and signing the term sheet, completing legal due diligence, drafting and signing the transaction documents, and receiving wired funds. The gap between term sheet and wire typically runs two to six weeks depending on the complexity of the round, the investor's diligence process, and how prepared the company's legal and financial materials are. If those materials are not in order when the term sheet arrives, the gap extends—and during that extension, the lead investor can re-trade terms or withdraw.

Continuing to advance other investor conversations until documents are signed protects you structurally. This is not a negotiating tactic. It is rational risk management. A lead who knows you have continued interest from other funds has less incentive to let the process drag. And if the unexpected does happen—a fund has an internal issue, a partner leaves, or due diligence surfaces something they were not expecting—you are not starting over from zero.

One thing that does not change at close: every investor relationship built during outreach stays yours. The investors who passed on this round, the ones who asked for follow-up after your next milestone, the ones who said "not right now but keep us updated"—those are future pipeline. The process you run now seeds the relationships you will work with across every future round.

## How to Run This Process While Also Running Your Company

The structural problem for most founders is that fundraising and operating compete for the same hours. Product decisions, customer calls, hiring, and investor outreach all require sustained focus. When fundraising pulls that focus, both suffer: outreach becomes inconsistent, investor follow-up slips, and the company's execution—which is what an investor is ultimately evaluating—visibly slows during the raise.

Options exist on a spectrum. At one end, the founder handles everything: a CRM, a database subscription, investor research, writing, and pipeline management, all self-managed. The economics are favorable, but the time cost is real, and the expertise gap is unforgiving. At the other end, success-fee advisors and placement agents typically charge a retainer plus a percentage of the raise and often run outreach under their own name, which keeps investor relationships with the advisor rather than the founder. Fundraising consultants provide advice and help with materials, but the founder still runs most of the outreach directly.

A managed outreach service that charges a setup fee and a flat monthly fee with no percentage of the raise sits in a different category. It addresses the time problem—because experienced operators do the research, writing, and follow-up—without the equity cost of a placement agent and without the relationship risk of outreach that goes out under someone else's name. When outreach comes from the founder's inbox, every conversation that starts belongs to the founder, regardless of what service helped initiate it.

Outsourcing the execution of this process is not an admission that you cannot do it. It is a recognition that fundraising is a specialized discipline, that your time has a cost, and that the opportunity cost of pulling a founder out of product and customer work for three to five months is real and measurable. The founders who raise fastest are rarely the ones who learned investor outreach from scratch mid-raise. They are the ones who entered the process prepared, targeted the right investors, and kept their calendar available for the conversations that matter—not for the research and writing that led to them.
